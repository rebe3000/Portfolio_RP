We set up a floating climate station to measure air temperature, water
temperature at three depths, wind speed, radiation intensity, atmospheric CO₂
concentration, and air pressure. Floating chambers were placed at five
locations in the lake, and long-term measurements of CO₂ concentration and
carbon fluxes were carried out at two-week intervals to calculate the exchange
coefficient.

We extracted sediment cores and carried out experiments on CH₄ and CO₂
production rates under aerobic and anaerobic conditions at two temperatures.

The main finding was that the lake acts as a methane source during spring and
overall as a CO₂ source. Emissions varied more over time rather than across
the lake. One central site near an area that became exposed as water levels
fell showed exceptionally high emissions, suggesting that shallowing and drying
zones may be emission hotspots. Methane emissions and dissolved CO₂ generally
increased during the study. Dissolved CO₂ was positively correlated with
temperature and solar radiation and negatively correlated with water depth.

Sediment from the lake centre and upper 0–10 cm layer had the highest potential
gas-production rates. Under aerobic conditions, sediment consumed methane;
under anaerobic conditions, it produced methane. Anaerobic methane production
was strongly temperature-sensitive (Q₁₀ = 1.64–3.52), indicating that warming
could increase methane production and emissions. The March–June study was too
short to characterise the full annual cycle; longer measurements would be
needed, particularly during summer drying.

This served as a preliminary study for later published works by the
aforementioned supervisors, in which I was not involved as a co-author but was
mentioned in the acknowledgements:
