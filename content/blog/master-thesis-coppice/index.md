Coppice management is a traditional form of woodland use that was once
widespread across Central Europe. In the Hauberge of central Germany,
cooperatively managed oak-and-birch woodlands are divided into small plots and
cut on a rotational cycle. This creates a mosaic of differently aged forest
stands. Recently cut plots receive abundant sunlight and provide habitat for
specialised plants and butterflies, including the rare ilex hairstreak
(*Satyrium ilicis*) and heath fritillary (*Melitaea athalia*).

For my MSc thesis, I investigated how woodland age and habitat characteristics
influence the occurrence of *S. ilicis*. I combined butterfly transect surveys
from two consecutive years with Distance Sampling, recorded vegetation and
habitat parameters in plots cut between one and ten years previously, and
developed generalised linear models to estimate butterfly density and identify
important habitat characteristics.

The highest predicted densities occurred four to five years after cutting,
reaching approximately 17 individuals per hectare. The main flight period
differed by one month between the two study years, and the importance of
individual habitat variables also varied. These results demonstrate that
recently cut and early-successional coppice stands provide important habitat
for light-demanding butterflies, while annual variation can limit the temporal
transferability of habitat models. Maintaining a rotational mosaic of forest
ages is therefore important for conserving these specialised species.

The field work was done by four students, two each year: Anne Graser, Meike
Schulz, Marit Kelling, and me.

This work resulted in the following article:
[Pabst et al. (2023), *Journal of Insect Conservation*](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10841-023-00494-8)
